# Data Used in Irrigation GIS

**Estimated reading time:** 15 minutes

## Introduction

Water is one of the world's most valuable resources, and agriculture consumes around 70% of global freshwater withdrawals. Efficient irrigation is therefore essential for improving crop productivity while conserving water. Modern irrigation management is no longer based solely on field experience—it increasingly relies on Geographic Information Systems (GIS).

GIS integrates multiple datasets into a single environment, allowing irrigation engineers, planners, farmers, and government agencies to understand where water comes from, how it moves, where it is needed, and how efficiently it is being used. The infographic highlights ten of the most important GIS datasets used in irrigation planning. This article explains each dataset, its purpose, and how they work together.

## Why GIS Matters in Irrigation

Irrigation systems involve terrain, soil, climate, crops, infrastructure, and water resources. No single dataset can answer every question. GIS combines these layers to support:

- Irrigation system design
- Water allocation
- Crop suitability analysis
- Infrastructure planning
- Drought monitoring
- Climate adaptation
- Asset management
- Decision support

## 1. Digital Elevation Model (DEM)

A DEM represents the Earth's surface elevation.

### Why it is important

Water naturally flows downhill. DEMs allow engineers to identify slopes, watersheds, drainage paths, and suitable canal alignments.

### Typical applications

- Slope analysis
- Flow direction
- Watershed delineation
- Gravity-fed irrigation planning
- Reservoir site selection

Without elevation information, irrigation design becomes inefficient and expensive.

## 2. Soil Map

Different soils retain water differently.

A soil map provides information about:

- Texture
- Drainage
- Water holding capacity
- Permeability
- Fertility

For example, clay soils retain water longer than sandy soils. This directly influences irrigation frequency and scheduling.

## 3. Land Use and Crop Distribution

Land-use data identifies how land is being utilized.

Examples include:

- Rice fields
- Vegetable farms
- Orchards
- Urban areas
- Forests

Each crop requires different amounts of water. GIS helps estimate total irrigation demand by combining crop type with cultivated area.

## 4. Canal Network

The canal network represents irrigation infrastructure including:

- Main canals
- Secondary canals
- Distributaries
- Control structures

GIS allows managers to locate damaged canals, estimate service areas, optimize maintenance, and evaluate expansion projects.

## 5. Rivers

Rivers are the primary natural water source for many irrigation systems.

GIS helps determine:

- Water availability
- Catchment relationships
- River connectivity
- Intake locations
- Flood impacts

River monitoring supports sustainable water extraction.

## 6. Reservoirs

Reservoirs regulate seasonal water supply.

GIS stores information about:

- Capacity
- Water level
- Storage volume
- Distribution zones

Combining reservoirs with rainfall forecasts improves drought preparedness.

## 7. Rainfall

Rainfall is one of the most dynamic datasets.

Rainfall maps help answer:

- Where is rainfall sufficient?
- Which areas require supplemental irrigation?
- Which regions are vulnerable to drought?

Satellite rainfall products provide near real-time monitoring over large areas.

## 8. Temperature

Temperature affects evapotranspiration (ET), which represents water lost through evaporation and plant transpiration.

Higher temperatures generally increase crop water demand.

Temperature maps are therefore essential for:

- Irrigation scheduling
- Crop water requirement estimation
- Climate impact assessment

## 9. Satellite Imagery

Satellite imagery provides continuous monitoring of agricultural land.

Common applications include:

- NDVI vegetation monitoring
- Crop stress detection
- Crop growth assessment
- Flood mapping
- Land-use change detection

Modern irrigation projects increasingly rely on free satellite missions such as Landsat and Sentinel.

## 10. GPS Survey

GPS surveys provide accurate field observations.

Typical GPS datasets include:

- Canal centerlines
- Water structures
- Farm boundaries
- Survey control points

These datasets ensure GIS analyses match real-world conditions.

## Integrating All Layers

The real power of GIS comes from combining datasets rather than analyzing them individually.

Example workflow:

1. DEM determines water flow.
2. Soil map estimates infiltration.
3. Land use estimates demand.
4. Canal network distributes water.
5. Reservoirs provide storage.
6. Rainfall updates supply.
7. Temperature estimates ET.
8. Satellite imagery monitors crop health.
9. GPS validates field conditions.

Together these datasets provide a complete decision-support system.

## Benefits

Organizations using GIS for irrigation can:

- Improve water-use efficiency
- Reduce operational costs
- Increase crop productivity
- Detect problems earlier
- Support precision agriculture
- Improve drought resilience
- Plan infrastructure investments
- Support sustainable resource management

## Future Trends

Emerging technologies are transforming irrigation GIS:

- IoT water sensors
- Drone imagery
- AI-based irrigation prediction
- Digital twins
- Cloud GIS
- Real-time dashboards

These technologies enable smarter and more automated irrigation systems.

## Conclusion

Irrigation GIS is much more than mapping canals. It integrates terrain, soils, climate, crops, infrastructure, satellite observations, and field surveys into a unified decision-support platform. Better data leads to better decisions, more efficient water use, higher agricultural productivity, and more sustainable irrigation management.

As agriculture faces climate change and increasing water scarcity, GIS will continue to play a central role in ensuring every drop of water is used wisely.
